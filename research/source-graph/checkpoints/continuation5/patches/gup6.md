## Kārtavīrya’s birth, guru remedy, divine body and parental reunion

PARTIAL — 72.1–47;73.1–22,27–28

Upāsanā 72.1–10 (gup6-event-001 / gup6-e-001). Śūrasena asks Indra for the detailed birth of the couple’s son after the vow. Indra says that through pleased Gaṇeśa’s favour the queen conceives and in the ninth month sees a son with two shoulders, beautiful face, nose and lotus eyes but no arms/hands, thighs/knees, lower legs or feet. She laments the gift, says remaining barren would have been better, questions removal of prior sin, the deity’s favour and Brahmin assurances, and repeatedly strikes her breast and forehead. The women weep; the king, ministers and townspeople hear and join the grief.

Upāsanā 72.11–20 (gup6-event-002 / gup6-e-002). The king questions Gaṇeśa’s compassion and declares his japa, tapas, remembrance, gifts, worship, Brahmin feeding, observance and homa apparently wasted. He says fate is stronger and effort fruitless, comparing the son with a mouse gained by breaking a mountain. Ministers urge him to cease grief because what must occur cannot be changed: they cite Rāma following the deer and Dharmarāja entering forbidden dice and then forest exile despite knowledge. Crying will not make the child beautiful; good unseen merit could allow his later improvement and kingship with time, as trees bear flowers and fruit. The king steadies himself and tells his wife to rise without grief. The king’s first verse is printed1 before the numbering resumes12; this span follows its sequential position.

Upāsanā 72.21–26 (gup6-event-003 / gup6-e-003). The king calls Brahmins, performs Gaṇeśa worship, svastivācana and auspicious śrāddha, and gives garlands, ornaments, clothes, cows and jewels. He gives clothes and appropriate gifts to friends, relatives, servants, musicians, bards, Cāraṇas and poor, blind and destitute people, distributing betel and sugar house to house. On the eleventh day he names the son Kārtavīrya and feeds the city with enthusiasm. Twelve years of the son’s life pass; Dattātreya voluntarily comes to his house.

Upāsanā 72.27–30 (gup6-event-004 / gup6-e-004). The king explicitly named Kṛtavīrya bows with his head at Dattātreya’s feet. The sage rises and embraces him. The king seats and honours him with seat, foot-water, arghya, a cow, clothes, sacred thread, incense, lamp, offerings, varied fruit, rubbing powder, betel, jewels, gold dakṣiṇā and foot-massage. When the sage is pleased and comfortably seated the king addresses him; the read-only fragment stops before the request.

Upāsanā 72.31–39 (gup6-event-005 / gup6-e-005). Kṛtavīrya says many births’ merit now bears fruit in seeing Dattātreya and predicts future good from his favour, since wrongdoers do not see such sages. Dattātreya explicitly says he came to see the king’s extraordinary son. The king again laments his apparently fruitless practices and describes the son as a thorn in his heart. He brings the boy; the sage covers his eyes, then knows the relevant action through meditation. He predicts this very son will conquer all and diagnoses the king’s failure to perform ācamana after yawning during the Sankaṣṭa vigil as dishonouring the distress-removing vow. The son was born without limbs but can become complete through a remedy. The king accepts the diagnosis and asks what method will restore his son through the sage’s favour.

Upāsanā 72.40–43 (gup6-event-006 / gup6-e-006). Compassionate Dattātreya teaches the CHILD the one-syllable mantra with its auxiliaries and prescribes devotional Gaṇeśa worship with it, fasting and one-meal rules. He predicts that after twelve years Gaṇeśa will give the child sight of himself and the deity’s mere glance will produce a divine body. Taking leave of the king, the sage disappears.

Upāsanā 72.44–47 (gup6-event-007 / gup6-e-007). After Dattātreya leaves, the footless son asks Kṛtavīrya to take him to a deep forest so he can practice for Gaṇeśa’s favour. The boy remains there, firmly resolved on tapas.

Upāsanā 73.1–6 (gup6-event-008 / gup6-e-008). The boy regularly japs the guru’s mantra, meditates Gaṇeśa with firm dedication, and lives on air without food like earth-clod or stone. Upāsanā 73.7–9 (gup6-event-009 / gup6-e-009). The explicitly named son of Kṛtavīrya bows and speaks before many sages in vimānas. He requests continual nondeviating devotion to the deity’s feet, saying he has no enthusiasm to ask anything else. Nevertheless, to satisfy his parents, he also asks beauty of body pleasing to all.

Upāsanā 73.10–15 (gup6-event-010 / gup6-e-010). Hearing the requests, māyā-bearing Gaṇeśa adopts aṇimā and enters the boy’s belly. With this entry Kṛtavīrya’s son gains a divine body with one thousand arms and two straight legs, standing like a mountain. Upāsanā 73.27–28 (gup6-event-014 / gup6-e-014). Sahasrabāhu honours the Brahmins and takes leave of the kings, then returns to his city to see his mother and father. Both parents and the city rejoice at his restored form. His parents give varied gifts to eminent Brahmins.

## Sundarī’s crime, rebirth, incidental vow and divine rescue

Searching house to house they find a fine vimāna and a severely diseased caṇḍālī with draining mouth, sores, insects, stench, wasted body and unclean appearance, whom Gaṇeśa’s messengers have come to take. Astonished, royal messengers bow to her and ask the divine servants who she was formerly, how she became like this and what merit accounts for her ascent.

Upāsanā 74.9–14 (gup6-event-017 / gup6-e-018). Divine messengers identify her as daughter of the Bengal kṣatriya Śāraṅgadhara, called Sundarā/Sundarī in the verse. She pursues adulterous encounters, costly clothing, ornaments and sensual pleasures, openly known in Bengal like a prostitute, repeatedly deceiving her husband Citra and spending her father’s amassed wealth.

Upāsanā 74.15–23 (gup6-event-018 / gup6-e-019). Royal messengers wait, seize her on return, bring her to the king and execute her outside at his command. Yama’s messengers take her to terrible hell, where she suffers upside down, bitten by worms and recalling former deeds. At a kalpa’s end she is born an unfortunate caṇḍālī in the mortal world. Upāsanā 74.38–45 (gup6-event-021 / gup6-e-022). The servants place her on the vimāna; she immediately gains divine radiance, clothing and ornaments and is taken toward Gaṇeśa amid divine music. Indra actually goes to Amarāvatī and all return to their places; she, with divine body and freed from sin through Sankaṣṭa merit, reaches Vaināyaka’s abode. Properly hearing or carefully telling this story gives all desired objects and removes distress.

Upāsanā 75.22–33 (gup6-event-024 / gup6-e-025). The messengers place the king, bearing Gaṇeśa’s form, aboard while people rejoice. They say pleased Vināyaka sent them, eager to see him. The attendants attempt upward movement, but the craft remains inert on earth. Some spot a leprous man and urge discarding him, saying the craft will rise when he descends. ## Budha’s family killings, rape-curse and leprous rebirth

PARTIAL — 76.1–62

Upāsanā 76.1–8 (gup6-event-026 / gup6-e-027). The messengers describe a learned ascetic Brahmin devoted to gods and Brahmins in Gauḍa city. His son’s mother is Śākinī; the son’s faithful wife is Sāvitrī. An only-child affection leads to adornments, great beauty compared with Rati’s husband, and parents unwilling to endure separation. In youth he abandons his wife for adultery, slander and disregard for paternal counsel. When an unnamed captivating prostitute comes, he puts away his ornaments before his parents, then steals them and gives them to her. He lives absorbed in her amid scents and pleasures, compared in dedication with a Brahman-focused yogin.

Upāsanā 76.9–18 (gup6-event-027 / gup6-e-028). The distressed father searches every house for his son without eating or drinking, but cannot see him. At midnight he tells his wife their home is empty without Budha and asks when life-preserving sight will return. Śākinī says hunger, thirst, anxiety and grief exhaust her and only seeing the boy will sustain her. Dūrva takes his staff and searches again. An elderly man of lowest caste named Bhīma tells him Budha is playing in the prostitute’s home, passion-bound and disregarding parentage, and says his grief is wasted. Dūrva asks how his son could become absorbed there and goes to that house.

Upāsanā 76.19–31 (gup6-event-028 / gup6-e-029). The father seizes the intoxicated, red-eyed son’s hand and rebukes him, preferring a thorn-tree or stone to such a son and questioning his life. Budha strikes the father’s face, insults him for obstructing his pleasure and kicks him to death.24 reports Hareśvara with so’vadat at the father’s death; its speaker is not assigned without support. The son rejoices, binds the father’s foot, throws the body away, drinks and returns to the prostitute. Next morning Śākinī joyfully embraces him, with milk flowing through affection, asks where he was, says both parents waited without food/water, and repeatedly urges him to find his searching father. Angered, he hits her head with dry wood, knows her unconscious, ties and throws her outside and returns to the prostitute. Good people cremate both parents with their wood. The king does not punish him because he is a Brahmin.21 is printed2.

Upāsanā 76.32–42 (gup6-event-029 / gup6-e-030). When Budha returns, wife Sāvitrī gently urges virtuous conduct suited to his respected birth: act now for future-world happiness, in youth for old age, during eight months for the rains and by day for the night. She says the king excuses him through affection for his father and his Brahmin status, asks why he abandoned his faithful, beautiful and compliant lawful wife for a prostitute, and describes public condemnation, fear of speaking to him and her shame. She proposes marital companionship rather than public wrongdoing, urges him to leave the prostitute and abandon ignorant or negligent deeds, and says continuing otherwise loses both this world and the next. Her words pierce Budha deeply.

Upāsanā 76.43–49 (gup6-event-030 / gup6-e-031). Enraged, Budha insults Sāvitrī and threatens her with his parents’ fate. He seizes her hair and beats her with wood, clods, fists and stones. Through prior merit she remembers Rāma in husband-form and dies from his blows to vital parts. She goes to heaven with divine body and enjoys highest happiness. He drags her by the foot and throws her far away at night, then tells the prostitute he destroyed everyone for her and resumes pleasure.

Upāsanā 76.50–62 (gup6-event-031 / gup6-e-032). After much time Budha visits sage Kalabha’s house while the sage bathes, seizes his beautiful wife by the hair, embraces, kisses and rapes her. He flees frightened to the prostitute but resumes drink and pleasure. Brahmā declines further wrongdoings, saying telling others’ faults diminishes merit. Budha eventually dies; Yama orders messengers to throw him into hell until the elemental dissolution. Having endured that, he is born in a Vaiśya home, severely leprous through the sage’s wife’s curse. The discourse condemns his deeds, prescribes clothed bathing after touching him and avoidance of his name, and says removing him will raise the craft.

## Jāmadagnya’s origin, hospitality/cow war, parent-loss and mother’s commands

Upāsanā 77.3–12 (gup6-event-035 / gup6-e-037). In Śvetadvīpa Jamadagni is a great sage, knowing three times and performing creation/destruction, restraint/favour through mind, frightening gods. His wife is explicitly Reṇukā, whose beauty exceeds Kāma’s wife.6 says she became famed as Rati; this source’s Reṇukā/Rati description is kept distinct from automatically identifying Kāma’s wife. Poetic comparisons describe creatures seeking her eye-beauty and Moon serving Īśvara for her face-beauty. She is praised as beginningless/endless goddess, root nature and ruler. Rāma is born to her from Jamadagni, here praised as an actual Īśvara form; the son is compared with Viṣṇu the yoga lord. He is beautiful, strong, obedient to parents, devoted to gods/Brahmins/gurus/wise people/cows/bodhi, learned in Vedas/Vedāṅgas/śāstras/smṛtis, eloquent as Bṛhaspati, forbearing as earth and deep as ocean.

Upāsanā 77.13–23 (gup6-event-036 / gup6-e-038). With his parents’ permission Rāma goes to the forest printed Nimeṣāraṇya, firmly intending study of many disciplines. After his departure mighty King Kārtavīrya appears. The source attributes earth-subjection, steady Lakṣmī as a Viṣṇu portion, five-hundred-arrow volleys and formidable strength to him. His innumerable elephants, chariots, horses and infantry cover earth when he moves; his conch frightens enemies, and five hundred hand-claps shake the cosmic egg. In blue clothes with blue parasol and similarly dressed four-part troops, he wanders forests, rivers, forts and mountains and kills some game. On a Sahyādri summit he sees an excellent hermitage and asks servants whose it is.

Upāsanā 77.24–33 (gup6-event-038 / gup6-e-040). The king’s servant identifies Jamadagni as a sage like a second Sun, capable of curse and favour, whose sight removes crores of sins, and advises a visit. Kārtavīrya holds back his full army, takes selected companions and goes to the sage sitting on kuśa with fire-like radiance. All bow; troops sit in large halls and groves while the king, with four attendants, sits on the sage’s appointed seat. Jamadagni and pupils honour them with foot-water, arghya, seats and cows. Hunt-worn troops bathe in clean cold lakes and hear pupils reciting Vedas and śāstras and debating.

Upāsanā 77.34–48 (gup6-event-039 / gup6-e-041). The king praises his parents, birth, knowledge, tapas, fortune, lineage and fame as fruitful through seeing Jamadagni and calls him Para-Brahman, satisfied by hospitality. Though knowing him, the sage asks identity, name and purpose. The king explicitly says he is Kṛtavīrya’s son called Kārtavīrya, with no purpose beyond beneficial sage sight, and asks leave to return. Jamadagni reciprocally praises the visit and insists he eat before departing. The king says he cannot even drink apart from innumerable troops and doubts all can be fed. The sage assures a four-kind meal for the whole army, invites even the home forces and asks him to rest briefly by the river while food is prepared.

Upāsanā 77.49–62 (gup6-event-040 / gup6-e-042). Astonished, the king goes to the riverbank. Jamadagni calls his wife, tells her what happened, and the couple summons and worships Kāmadhenu. They ask her to preserve their honour and truth by instantly feeding the invited king’s innumerable forces according to taste, otherwise their promise would fail and ill fame follow. At their prayer she creates by her own power a great city with beautiful houses, gem-pillared halls, gardens, flags, music, vessel rows, four-kind food, gates and moat, male/female attendants and abundant prepared meals. Guards prevent unauthorized entry under Jamadagni’s rule. Varied soups, pāyasa, cooked dishes, pañcāmṛta, edible/lickable/suckable/drinkable foods, fruits and seasoned dishes are prepared in innumerable bright vessels. Through Kāmadhenu’s satisfaction, the sage summons pupils to invite Kṛtavīrya’s son and troops to eat.

Upāsanā 78.1–8 (gup6-event-041 / gup6-e-043). Jamadagni urgently sends pupils to invite the riverbank king, with blessed greetings and prepared innumerable six-taste dishes. Kārtavīrya and the army bathe and come to a house and gateway he has neither seen nor heard of in three worlds. Staff-bearing guards initially stop him, seeking the sage’s signal, but pupils overrule them and bring him into the hall. The king inwardly wonders at wealth exceeding even Hari, Hara and Brahmā. The sage comes forward and seats guests at their vessels.

Upāsanā 78.9–21 (gup6-event-042 / gup6-e-044). With the king’s permission guests and troops, including outside diners, are seated in rows; the king is seated after everyone. They eat amid music, question unfamiliar tasty dishes, roots and fruits and marvel at preparation within one watch. Pupils give washing-water and tooth-cleaning sticks and leftovers to elephants, horses and bulls. In another large prepared hall people eat sugarcane, grapes, mango, jackfruit and pomegranate and receive cardamom, clove, camphor, khadira/areca and betel. Jamadagni gives everyone suitable clothes and ornaments and especially costly gifts to the king. He modestly asks what a forest sage could provide a king already fulfilling all wishes and praised as a Viṣṇu form; by following his invitation the king has made his fame and the sage’s successful hospitality known, as a great man obeying a smaller man gains saintly reputation.

Upāsanā 78.22–32 (gup6-event-043 / gup6-e-045). Astonished, the king asks whether the previously unseen marvel arose through māyā or tapas. Jamadagni says he has never uttered falsehood, even in jest, and truthfully identifies Kāmadhenu as the agent of everything. Brahmā remarks on adverse fate and approaching ill fortune. Though fully fed, the king decides to take the cow. He argues peaceful, desireless, sense-controlled forest sages living on roots, fruit or air, studying Vedas/śāstras and seeking liberation have no need for such riches; a great object belongs with great work and a forest dweller is not suited to the jewel. He demands a willing gift of Kāmadhenu and says she will still be the sage’s when kept with him, then threatens what powerful kings with armies cannot seize at home or abroad.

Upāsanā 78.33–38 (gup6-event-044 / gup6-e-046). Hearing Kārtavīrya’s words, Jamadagni burns with anger like Fire fed ghee and rebukes him with red eyes. He says he invited what he thought a good, pure, great king but did not know hidden crookedness, using heron and cuckoo/crow comparisons. He condemns his own error in seeking royal friendship, a friendship neither seen, heard nor experienced in the world.

Upāsanā 79.1–8 (gup6-event-045 / gup6-e-047). Jamadagni says the king seeks Kāmadhenu while courting self-destruction, desires the unattainable and risks the sin of destroying three worlds. The king erupts with anger and says only the sage’s Brahmin status made him endure harsh words. He rises and orders messengers to release the cow from her tether and bring her. They surround her, but her foṭkāra blast kills some; her anger-fire burns others. The force of her breath blows other warriors into the sky, covering the Sun and making the scene obscure.

Upāsanā 79.9–18 (gup6-event-046 / gup6-e-048). Darkness, earth-shaking and falling trees accompany the assault. Troops flee in ten directions. Someone whips the cow from a distance; she leaps and chases the army like lion against elephants or Garuḍa against serpents. Kārtavīrya tells frightened troops he will blow his conch and make her flee, but the mighty world-filling blast does not frighten her. Servants then strike her with staffs and iron. From every struck body location emerge armed, equipped warriors. Śakas and Barbaras arise from her hair; Paṭaccaras from her foot region, with varied Yavana peoples and other warriors also arising. Cavalry, elephants and powerful charioteers join combat against the king’s troops.

Upāsanā 79.19–28 (gup6-event-047 / gup6-e-049). Cow-generated fighters strike down royal troops; more converge and hundreds die through mutual blows. Weapons smash weapons, then wrestling continues amid dust, noise, obscured Sun and inability to distinguish one’s own side. Horses, elephants, chariot wheels, drums, flutes and war cries fill the battle, frightening spirits/Rākṣasas and warriors’ wives while benefiting scavengers. Knees and heads are broken; swords, shields, bhallas, arrows, bows, warriors and chariots fall beyond count. Remaining royal soldiers flee. Cow troops pursue laughing but DO NOT kill the fugitives, rebuking them for attacking a sage who harmed them in no way. The narrator attributes the king’s bad judgment to former fault.

Upāsanā 79.29–34 (gup6-event-048 / gup6-e-050). With his army broken, Kṛtavīrya’s son rises, takes FIVE HUNDRED ARROWS and bows, places his left knee on earth, draws the bows and shoots an arrow-net at the cow army. The number grammatically accompanies arrows; no exact five-hundred-bow count is asserted. The volley is fruitless. Repeated equal volleys break not even one cow warrior. The king despairs at his lost strength. The cow army says one should not strike a distressed opponent and goes to heaven; Kāmadhenu questions combat with the lesser opponent.30 is printed00.

Upāsanā 79.35–43 (gup6-event-049 / gup6-e-051). After Kāmadhenu leaves, Kārtavīrya goes to Jamadagni in anger, claims to know his deceit and says a deceitful man should not count as a Brahmin. The KING HIMSELF takes one arrow and pierces the sage; the arrow lodges in his heart and Jamadagni gives up life. Reṇukā protests needless Brahmin-killing. The red-eyed king tells her to be silent or he will kill her too and strikes her with TWENTY-ONE arrows. She remembers Jamadagni and remains alive to ask why he struck innocent parents and predict certain destruction of his arms. Hearing her, the king departs to his city with a small remaining army, anxious, grieving soldiers, reproaching himself and without enthusiasm. This chapter describes a continuous visit sequence;80.21’s later recollection gives a different home-and-return chronology.

Upāsanā 80.1–14 (gup6-event-050 / gup6-e-052). After the king leaves, the arrow-covered Reṇukā grieves, asking where her sons are and especially her angry beloved Rāma, and says seeing him will precede her departure to the gods’ abode. At her remembrance Rāma comes, sees her arrows and his father dead from Kārtavīrya’s heart-arrow, faints like a wind-felled tree and weeps. He compares the fatherless hermitage with earth lacking Meru, Amarāvatī without Indra and the world without Gaṅgā, laments the parents’ great tapas and mother’s world-burning capacity, and removes her arrows with her head on his lap. Recalling her milk, food, clothing, fruit and attentive care, he condemns life without his parents. The plural sons are unnamed; no names or counts of brothers are imported.

Upāsanā 80.15–22 (gup6-event-051 / gup6-e-053). Reṇukā wipes her son’s tears, says she will remain near him and tells the earlier events: Kṛtavīrya’s son arrived at noon with troops, was honoured and fed through Kāmadhenu, then demanded the cow. When the sage was silent he angrily released her; at contact she generated a powerful four-part army. Royal troops fled and the king repeatedly loosed five hundred arrows but was defeated. She explicitly says he WENT HOME, the cow went to heaven, and he RETURNED to kill the father with one chest-arrow and wound her without offence with twenty-one. This direct mother’s recollection differs from79.35–43’s continuous visit sequence; both are retained.

Upāsanā 80.23–27 (gup6-event-052 / gup6-e-054). Reṇukā commands prompt destruction of the wicked king and making earth without kṣatriyas TWENTY-ONE times because twenty-one arrows were put in her body. She gives another instruction: perform the parents’ funeral rites where no one has ever been cremated, summon all-Veda-knowing Dattātreya, and perform subsequent rites through thirteen days so both attain their destination; no other teacher is equivalent. After speaking she actually abandons her body and reaches a difficult-to-attain abode. The narrator adds that Rāma did all she commanded. That concise fulfillment summary is retained without inventing the unexamined detailed funeral or repeated revenge episodes.
