Why Would the Remover of Obstacles Ever Create One?

Two Purāṇas describe Ganesha not only removing obstacles, but placing them.

← Ganapati: Beyond the Obstacle Remover

Season homeThe Eight ManifestationsVighnahartā & VighnakartāThe Eight Obstacles

Almost everyone meets Ganesha as Vighnahartā, the remover of obstacles. Two texts describe something stranger and, once you have read them, harder to forget: he is Vighneśa, lord of obstacles — which includes placing them.

The Skanda Purāṇa: the lord who regulates obstacles

Mūla · what the text says

In the Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa, chapter 27 gives an origin account of Ganesha. Pārvatī creates a form with an elephant face from the material of her own cleansing. Śiva then describes what this son will do, and the passage is explicit in both directions. Those who hate the Vedas and dharma will be harassed by obstacles; those who do not honour what is worthy of reverence, who are given to anger and untruth, will be hindered. And he will remove the obstacles of those who abide by dharma, who keep the customs of their community, who are merciful and free from anger. The passage adds that without his worship, pious rites of every kind are obstructed. A few lines later the dual role becomes concrete: praised by the devas, Vighnapati makes their activities obstacle-free while obstructing the activities of the daityas.

The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa: Vyāsa is obstructed because knowledge became pride

Mūla · what the text says

The better story sits in the Upāsanā-khaṇḍa. Vyāsa is enormously learned — he has divided the Vedas, and he begins compiling the Purāṇas so that ordinary people can understand them. Then his work stops. His knowledge no longer carries him; the text says he became confused and inactive, like a snake whose poison has been neutralised, and could not understand the cause of his own condition. He goes to Brahma to ask why. The answer is not that he lacks intelligence. It is that he relied on the power of his own wisdom, became arrogant, and did not worship or even remember Ganesha before beginning. Brahma states the principle plainly: he removes obstacles for his devotees, but creates obstacles for others. Vyāsa then asks who Ganesha truly is — and that question opens the rest of the Purāṇa.

What we take from it

Drishti · our reading

The reading that stays with us is this: the most dangerous obstacle may not arrive when you are weak. It may arrive when you have become competent enough to believe you no longer need to begin with humility. Vyāsa is not a beginner who fails. He is the most capable person in the story, stopped by the one thing capability cannot supply. That sentence is our interpretation. What the sources supply is the narrative of pride and obstruction — and the striking fact that a deity invoked at every beginning is also described as the one who can withhold a beginning.
