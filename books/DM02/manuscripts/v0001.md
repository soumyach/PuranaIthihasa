# The Sleep That Held Vishnu

*A Story of Yoganidra, Brahma, Vishnu, Madhu and Kaitabha*

## The Sleep Beyond the World

Sage Medhas began with a distinction.

“The Goddess is eternal,” he said. “When the sacred account speaks of her appearing, it tells of the forms by which she becomes known for divine purposes.”

Then his story moved beyond kingdoms, roads and remembered seasons, to the dissolution of a world.

Water stretched through the vastness. No shore divided one direction from another. There was no field to cross, no mountain path to climb, no city lamp to mark a home. The flood of dissolution held the scene in a silence deeper than ordinary night.

Upon Shesha rested Vishnu.

He was not keeping the light sleep of a traveller who might wake at the snap of a twig. Yoganidra, the divine power of cosmic sleep, held him in profound stillness. His breathing alone gave measure to the immeasurable quiet.

From him rose a lotus, and upon that lotus sat Brahma.

The flower stood above the waters like the single open eye of a world not yet remade. Brahma had no ground beneath him—only the lotus, Vishnu below, and the unbroken flood beyond.

Vishnu slept.

The waters waited.

And in that stillness, danger appeared.

## The Danger at the Lotus

From the region of Vishnu’s ears arose two Asuras: Madhu and Kaitabha.

The account gives no childhood for them, no family history and no earlier boon. They entered this story already powerful, moving through the waters of dissolution toward the lotus where Brahma sat.

Their purpose was plain. They threatened to kill him.

Brahma looked from the approaching figures to Vishnu. The preserver still rested upon Shesha, held in the depth of Yoganidra. No call from the lotus had yet broken that sleep.

Madhu and Kaitabha came nearer.

There was nowhere for Brahma to flee. Around the lotus lay the same endless flood. Below it slept the one who could face the threat. Yet the sleep upon Vishnu was itself divine power, not an ordinary weariness that Brahma could simply shake away.

The danger and the answer stood almost together: the threatening Asuras upon the waters, Vishnu beneath the lotus, and the power of sleep holding him still.

Brahma did not address the sleepers with a hurried shout. He turned his praise toward Yoganidra herself.

The silence at the end of the world became a place of invocation.

## Brahma’s Invocation

Brahma gathered his attention and praised the Goddess who held Vishnu in sleep.

“You are the sacred utterance by which offerings are made,” he said. “You are the power by which creation arises, by which it is preserved, and by which it is drawn again into dissolution. You are knowledge, and you are the great illusion that can veil knowledge. You are nature, nourishment and peace. You are also power armed against danger.”

These were Brahma’s words of praise within the sacred story. They named Yoganidra through many cosmic and sacred functions; they were not a list invented by the storyteller, nor a declaration added by this book.

Madhu and Kaitabha continued their approach.

Brahma’s praise became a direct appeal.

“Great Goddess, delude these two Asuras. Withdraw your sleep from Vishnu and awaken him, so that their threat may be ended.”

The lotus did not move away from danger. The waters did not open a road of escape. Nothing in the scene became smaller or safer.

But the invocation had found the power to whom it was addressed.

The change began within the stillness of Vishnu himself.

## When Sleep Stood Apart

Yoganidra withdrew from the places named in the account: from Vishnu’s eyes, mouth and nose; from his arms, heart and breast.

The withdrawal was not described as the loss of a mere shadow. The power that had held Vishnu in cosmic sleep now appeared before Brahma.

For one suspended moment, the whole scene had changed without the waters changing at all. Brahma remained upon the lotus. Madhu and Kaitabha remained near. Shesha still bore Vishnu above the flood.

Yet sleep no longer enclosed him.

Vishnu’s eyes opened.

Stillness gave way to attention. Rest gave way to readiness. He saw Brahma upon the lotus and the two Asuras who had come to destroy him.

Vishnu rose to meet Madhu and Kaitabha.

No army marched into the waters. No ranks gathered behind him. The source tells of a combat fought with his arms, close and unrelenting.

The two Asuras turned from the lotus to face him.

Brahma’s danger had not vanished; it had become a confrontation. Yoganidra had answered the invocation by withdrawing, appearing before Brahma, and opening the way for Vishnu to wake.

Now the long struggle began.

## Five Thousand Years

Vishnu grappled with Madhu and Kaitabha upon the cosmic waters.

A hold was made and broken. One Asura drove forward; Vishnu checked him. The other closed from the side; Vishnu turned to meet him. Again they joined, separated and returned.

Years passed, yet the struggle did not end.

Hundreds became thousands.

For five thousand years, Vishnu fought the two Asuras with his arms.

The story’s measure of time is immense, but its problem is easy to understand: strength met strength, and still the danger remained. Whenever one attacker was forced back, the other pressed in. Whenever both were driven away, they returned to the struggle.

Then Yoganidra’s power brought the reversal Brahma had requested.

Madhu and Kaitabha became deluded. Intoxicated with their own strength, they no longer saw the contest clearly. They had fought Vishnu for five thousand years, and pride made them speak as if the advantage were theirs to give.

Madhu drew back.

“Ask a boon of us,” he said.

Kaitabha stood beside him. “Yes. Choose what we shall give.”

After an age of combat, the strangest moment in the battle was not another blow. It was the offer made by the two who had come as enemies.

They had become the boon-givers.

Vishnu answered them.

## The Boon Turned Back

“Then let the boon be this,” Vishnu said. “Let both of you be overcome by me.”

The words changed the shape of the contest.

Madhu and Kaitabha had offered the boon jointly. Vishnu had not asked them for land, treasure or long life. He had asked for the one outcome their pride had not expected to grant: their own defeat at his hands.

Across the scene lay only the flood of dissolution.

Madhu answered, “Only where earth is not flooded.”

Kaitabha added, “On that condition, we grant it.”

It was a condition attached by both demons to the boon they had offered. The account does not bring in another deity to grant it, nor does it tell of an older ascetic bargain that made the demons invulnerable. Here the boon began with Madhu and Kaitabha, and its condition came from them.

Vishnu replied, “I accept.”

The combat had lasted five thousand years. The exchange took only a few words. Yet those words did what force alone had not done: they fixed a condition under which the struggle could end.

The waters were still everywhere around them.

Vishnu met the condition.

## Where the Earth Was Not Flooded

The exact bodily place associated with the final act is uncertain across the bounded witnesses used for this book. The story therefore keeps only what the cleared evidence establishes: the place of defeat met the demons’ condition, for the earth there was not under the flood.

Vishnu raised his discus.

Its swift circle ended the contest. Madhu and Kaitabha were killed by Vishnu’s hand, as the boon allowed. The telling does not linger over the violence. It is enough to know that the two who had threatened Brahma could no longer reach the lotus.

After five thousand years of struggle, there was no third attacker and no further condition. The unusual boon had been offered, chosen, limited and fulfilled.

Brahma remained safe upon the lotus.

Vishnu was awake.

Yoganidra’s part in the episode was complete: she had held the cosmic sleep, withdrawn when Brahma invoked her, appeared before him, and deluded the two Asuras until their confidence turned the battle.

The waters of dissolution still belonged to a story larger than this one. This bounded tale does not claim when they receded or what came next.

Sage Medhas brought the episode to its close where the source-bounded action closes: the threat to Brahma ended, Madhu and Kaitabha defeated under their own condition, and the power behind sleep revealed through what she had done.

The story was complete.

## Editorial Reflection — A Thought for Families

This is a modern editorial reflection, not a quotation from the Devi Mahatmya and not an additional event in the sacred story.

The episode asks readers to notice how its turning points differ from one another. Brahma cannot solve the danger by pretending Vishnu’s sleep is ordinary; he addresses Yoganidra, the power holding that sleep. Vishnu’s awakening begins the struggle, but even immense strength does not bring a quick ending. The final reversal comes when Madhu and Kaitabha, deluded and proud of their strength, offer a boon and then state its condition.

The story does not treat sleep, strength and wisdom as if they were the same thing. Each has a different place in the sequence. Yoganidra’s withdrawal makes awakening possible. Vishnu’s strength sustains the long confrontation. The boon exchange creates the condition that lets the danger end.

A family might ask: When a problem will not yield to force, what condition is keeping it in place? What changes when that condition is finally understood?

Those questions are offered for conversation. They are not presented as words spoken by Medhas, Brahma, Vishnu, Madhu, Kaitabha or the Goddess.
