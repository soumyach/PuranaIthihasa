# The Buffalo King

*The Divine Mother and Mahishasura*

## 1. The Empty Thrones

In the forest hermitage, evening arrived without hurry. Light rested on the leaves, and the last birds crossed the sky above Sage Medhas. The sage waited until the listening world had grown still.

"A kingdom is not lost only when its gates are broken," he began. "Sometimes it is lost when every rightful place has been taken. Listen, and I will tell you of a time when even the heavens stood empty."

For one hundred years, the gods had fought the armies of Mahishasura. The struggle had stretched beyond a single campaign, beyond the memory of one season following another. At last the buffalo-shaped Asura king prevailed. He took the office of Indra and claimed the powers and duties that belonged to the rulers of the sky, fire, wind, moon and waters. The gods who had once held their stations wandered upon the earth with no place to return to.

Defeat had done more than scatter an army. It had broken the order by which each divine power served the worlds. They gathered, and Brahma led them to Shiva and Vishnu.

"Mahishasura holds our places," the gods said. "He commands where we once served. Our strength has not been enough. Help us find the means by which he can be stopped."

Anger rose in Shiva and Vishnu. From their faces blazed a fierce radiance. Light streamed from Brahma, Indra and the other gods as well.

Each brightness was distinct as it left its source. But none remained separate. Above the gathered gods, the many radiances moved toward one another. They joined, intensified and became too brilliant to look upon directly. What the gods could not accomplish as a defeated host was taking a new form before them.

Medhas lowered his voice.

"Their loss was not the end of the story," he said. "It was the moment when scattered power began to become one."

## 2. Light Becomes a Warrior

The gathered brilliance rose like a mountain of flame. Then its outline changed. Within the light appeared a face, hair, arms, hands and feet. The radiance of Shiva formed her face; Yama's became her hair; Vishnu's became her arms. The moon's light formed her breast, Indra's her waist, and Varuna's her thighs and legs. The Earth's power formed her hips, and Brahma's her feet. Sunlight shone in her toes, the Vasus' radiance in her fingers. Kubera's became her nose, Prajapati's her teeth and Agni's her eyes. The two twilights formed her brows, and the wind's power formed her ears.

Where many lights had met, Devi now stood. She was not an ornament for the gods' despair. She was its answer.

The gods looked upon her with joy, and one by one they placed in her hands the means of battle. Shiva drew a trident from his own. Vishnu gave a discus. Varuna offered a conch and a noose; Agni, a spear. Vayu brought a bow and two quivers filled with arrows. Indra gave his thunderbolt and a bell from Airavata, his great elephant. Yama gave a rod. Prajapati placed prayer beads in her hand, and Brahma gave a water-pot. The Sun poured his rays into every pore of her skin. Kala gave a sword and shield.

Still the gifts continued. The Ocean of Milk brought a necklace of pearls and garments that would not fade. Ornaments shone at her crown, ears, wrists, arms, ankles and fingers. Vishvakarman gave her an axe, other weapons and armor described as impenetrable. The Ocean offered unfading lotus garlands and a lotus for her hand. Himavat, lord of the mountains, brought jewels and a lion to carry her. Kubera offered a cup filled with drink, and Shesha, lord of serpents, gave a necklace set with gems.

The moment was solemn, but it was not still. Each gift changed what could happen next. The bow gave reach; the noose could restrain; the bell could bewilder an oncoming force. Sword, shield, spear, discus, thunderbolt and trident waited for different turns in the struggle. The lion crouched beneath Devi, strength gathered in every limb.

The gods had not placed their hopes in a stranger. The same powers by which they acted in the worlds now shone together before them, held without confusion in Devi's many hands.

The gods had asked for a way to end Mahishasura's rule. Before them, the warrior born from their radiance stood armed and ready.

Devi lifted her head and roared.

## 3. The Roar Across the Worlds

Her roar did not end at the edge of the gathering. It rolled through the worlds. The sea heaved. The earth shook, and mountains trembled as the sound passed through them. The gods answered with cries of victory, while sages bowed in praise.

Far away, Mahishasura heard the disturbance.

He called his forces and moved toward its source. Chariots crowded the roads. Elephants advanced beside cavalry, and ranks of foot soldiers filled the ground between them. At their head came his commanders. Cikshura led one great division; Chamara came with another. Udagra, Mahahanu, Asiloman, Bashkala and Bidala brought immense contingents armed for both distant and close combat. The movement of so vast a host sounded like a storm building upon itself.

Then the Asura army saw Devi.

Her radiance filled the three worlds. Her crown touched the sky. When she drew her bow, its string rang through the depths below and the heights above. Her countless arms held the gods' gifts without strain. Beneath her, the lion watched the approaching army.

For a breath, the two forces faced one another. The Asuras had driven the gods from heaven; now they stood before Devi.

Cikshura raised his weapon and signaled the first attack. The sky darkened beneath a flight of missiles. Spears, arrows and other weapons rushed toward Devi from so many directions that they seemed to weave a roof above the field.

Devi answered at once. Her own weapons moved in bright, exact paths. Arrow met arrow. Blade turned missile. What had been launched as an overwhelming storm fell broken before it could reach her.

The missile barrage from Mahishasura's army broke before Devi. Broken weapons fell before the front ranks. But the commanders pressed them onward, and the distance between the armies vanished.

Devi's lion sprang.

## 4. An Army from Breath

The lion struck the Asura ranks with the force of a forest fire driven by wind. Where soldiers closed around him, he broke their formation. Where the line opened, he drove through it. Devi remained at the center of the battle, cutting down each new rain of weapons before it could gather strength.

Then every breath she released became a warrior. They sprang into being already charged with her power. Some carried axes, some javelins, some swords and some halberds. Drums sounded among them; conches answered. An army no commander had counted now stood beside the lion.

The Asura ranks closed again. Devi's noose caught those who rushed too near, and her other weapons struck through the massed force. When she sounded Indra's bell, its great voice rolled over the field and confounded the enemy. Their movement faltered and their formations broke apart while the breath-born troops pressed forward.

The field grew crowded with the fallen, with some terrible figures still moving as if the rhythm of combat carried them on. The way became blocked, and streams ran red across the churned ground. Mahishasura's vast host was being destroyed.

The lion struck where resistance remained. The bell confounded the enemy; the noose held them; arrows and blades ended their attacks. The Asura host could no longer move as one.

At last, the massed army that had covered the field could no longer stand. Devi, her lion and the troops born from her breath had broken it. From above, the gods showered flowers upon her.

But the commanders were not all gone. Through the wreck of his army, Cikshura came forward. His bow was ready, and this time every arrow was aimed at Devi herself.

The field grew quiet enough to hear the first string snap.

## 5. The Last Commanders

Cikshura fired with the speed of a storm. Devi cut his arrows in flight. He sent more; she answered again, not allowing a single volley to change her position. Then she turned from defense to decision. Her arrows brought down his horses and charioteer, broke his bow, split his banner and wounded him. In moments, the chariot from which he had commanded an army was useless.

Cikshura seized a sword and shield and charged on foot. He struck the lion, then swung at Devi's arm. The sword broke against her. Still he did not withdraw. He hurled his spear with all the force he had left. Devi cast her own spear to meet it. His weapon shattered, and the same answering force ended his attack. Cikshura fell.

Chamara came next, mounted upon an elephant. From that height he threw a spear down at Devi. She met it with a single powerful sound, and the weapon lost its force before it reached her. Chamara snatched up another spear, but Devi's arrows cut it apart.

Her lion leaped onto the elephant. Lion and Asura struggled from its back to the ground, neither giving room. Chamara recovered and turned to meet him. The lion sprang upward, and one final blow of his paw struck off Chamara's head. The elephant stood without its rider; another commander was gone.

Elsewhere on the field, the remaining chiefs attacked in waves rather than waiting for single combat. Devi met each according to the danger before her. Udagra advanced through a storm of stones and trees, and there he was overcome. Karala came close enough to fight hand to hand; Devi stopped him with the force of her own limbs. Uddhata fell beneath her club, and Bashkala beneath the heavy bhindipala. Her arrows ended the attacks of Tamra and Andhaka.

Three more—Ugrasya, Ugravirya and Mahahanu—drove forward together. The trident answered them. Bidala charged after them and met Devi's sword. Durdhara and Durmukha tried to hold their distance, but her arrows found them before theirs could turn the battle.

Name after name had once meant another division entering the field. Now each name marked a command that could no longer be given. The long list of Mahishasura's forces had narrowed to one remaining power.

The ground shuddered. It was not the tread of another army.

Mahishasura himself had entered the fight.

## 6. The Buffalo Enters

He came in buffalo form, vast and furious. His muzzle swept warriors from their feet. His hooves struck the ground; his tail lashed; his horns drove through every attempt to surround him. When the troops born from Devi's breath closed in, he wheeled among them, scattering them with his rush and the force of his breath. His roar shook the field.

The lion moved to intercept him. Mahishasura turned upon the great beast, and the clash between them made the earth tremble. With his horns the buffalo tossed mountains. The sea was troubled, clouds were scattered and the peaks themselves shook beneath the force of his movement. This was no commander fighting after his army. It was the Asura king using his own body as a battlefield.

Devi cast Varuna's noose and bound him.

The bound buffalo vanished. A lion tore free in its place.

Devi raised her sword to cut down the new form. Before the blade completed its path, the lion became a man holding sword and shield. Her arrows struck the man and broke his weapons. He changed again.

An elephant towered over the field. Its trunk caught Devi's lion and pulled him close. She cut the trunk, releasing her mount. Before the elephant could fall, its shape surged outward and Mahishasura stood once more as the buffalo.

The noose held a buffalo; a lion appeared. As Devi raised her sword against the lion, an armed man rose. Her arrows broke the man's defense; an elephant seized her lion. When Devi cut the elephant's trunk, the buffalo returned.

Devi lifted Kubera's cup, drank and laughed.

Mahishasura tore mountains from the earth and hurled them at her. Devi's arrows shattered them in the sky. Stone burst into harmless fragments before it could reach her.

## 7. The Shape That Could Not Escape

The buffalo king roared and thundered across the field.

Devi did not step back.

"Roar while you can, Mahishasura," she said. "When this battle is ended, the gods will raise their voices."

She leaped, came down upon him and pressed his neck beneath her foot. The force that had shaken earth and mountain was held against the ground.

Devi struck him with the trident. Mahishasura began to leave the buffalo form, pushing halfway out through its mouth. Before he could emerge fully, she cut off his head with one stroke and ended the struggle.

The enormous buffalo shape went still. The roar that had driven the worlds into confusion ceased.

Silence held the field for one breath. Then the heavens answered. The gods cried out in joy. Celestial singers filled the air with song, and dancers moved in celebration. Flowers fell through the clear space where weapons had crossed moments before.

The last stroke completed a long chain of battles: an army had given way to commanders, commanders to a king, and the king had shifted from buffalo to lion, man and elephant before returning to buffalo again. Devi had cut missiles, broken the army, defeated its commanders, restrained the changing king and stopped his final emergence.

The gods' places had not been restored by another century of the same failed war. The gathered radiance they had witnessed at the beginning had moved through every gift, every counter and every turn of the battle to this ending.

Mahishasura was dead. His army no longer held the heavens. The story's central conflict was complete.

But victory left the gods with something more to say than a cheer. They approached Devi together, their heads bowed.

## 8. After the Thunder

The battlefield quieted. The gods stood before Devi and spoke a hymn of praise.

"You are the power present through the worlds," they said. "You are knowledge and the fortune that sustains a household. You protect those who seek refuge. Your greatness is beyond the measure of our speech."

Their hymn also praised mercy within the battle.

"Even those struck down by you have reached heaven through meeting you in battle," they said. "Guard us with your gentle form and with your fearsome form. Let your trident, sword, bell and bow protect the directions around us."

The field still bore the cost of the war. The gods offered flowers, fragrant perfumes and incense.

Devi received their worship and invited them to name a boon. They made one request.

"When calamity comes and we remember you, relieve us," they asked. "And may mortals who sing this hymn with devotion receive prosperity."

Devi answered, "What you have asked shall be granted."

Then she disappeared from their sight.

The gods remained beneath a sky no longer crowded by Mahishasura's weapons. They had begun as exiles, wandering after their places were taken. They had sought help, witnessed their powers gather as Devi, armed her, watched the defeat of the army that had driven them out and bowed after its king had fallen. Now the roar of war was gone. Incense rose where dust had covered the air.

Sage Medhas let the forest silence return around his own listeners.

"That is how Mahishasura's rule was broken," he said. "The gods asked for aid in calamity, Devi granted their request, and she vanished from their sight."

The buffalo king's story was finished.

## After the Story

EDITORIAL REFLECTION

A threat may change its shape without changing the harm it causes. In this story, strength matters, but so do attention and right response. Devi does not answer every danger in the same way: she cuts missiles, breaks an army's coordination, meets commanders one by one, binds the charging buffalo and counters each new form. This is a modern Khatakshetra reflection on the narrative, not a quotation from the Devi Mahatmya and not a new doctrine added to it.

A ROAD BEYOND THIS VOLUME

Only after the victory, praise, boon and disappearance were complete did Sage Medhas turn toward another episode. He spoke of a later time when the Goddess would emerge from Gauri and another danger would confront the gods. That belongs to a different story. The forest listeners settled closer, ready to hear it when the sage began again.
