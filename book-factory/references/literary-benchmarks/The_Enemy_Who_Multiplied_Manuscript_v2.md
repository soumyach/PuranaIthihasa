# THE ENEMY WHO MULTIPLIED

### A Story of the Divine Mother and the Seven Powers

**Khatakshetra Kathas · Revised family-reading manuscript · v2.0**

**Based mainly on:** *Devi Mahatmya* (also called *Durga Saptashati*), chapters 5–9, within the *Markandeya Purana*. The traditional framing story comes from chapter 1, and the connection to autumn worship is described in chapter 12.

**A note for families:** This is an illustrated-storybook manuscript, not a literal verse-by-verse translation. The important events and outcome follow the sacred text. Some simple conversations, reactions, and scene transitions are literary adaptations, not words quoted directly from the scripture. The narrative presents battles and danger without dwelling on graphic violence.

> **What would you do if every time you struck an enemy, ten more appeared?**
>
> That is the problem the Divine Mother and her companions must solve in this story.

---

## Chapter 1 · The King Who Couldn't Stop Worrying

Long ago, in a quiet forest, a king came to visit a wise sage named **Medhas**.

The king's name was **Suratha**. He had lost his kingdom and been betrayed by people he once trusted. He was safe in the forest now. But his mind was not at peace.

He still wondered whether his horses were being cared for. Was his treasury safe? What had happened to his people? Why did he still think about those who had hurt him?

At the hermitage he met **Samadhi**, a merchant whose family had sent him away because they wanted his wealth. The merchant, too, kept worrying about those who had mistreated him.

“Why do I still care?” the king asked him.

“I ask myself the same thing,” the merchant replied. “Knowing what happened has not stopped my heart from worrying.”

Together they approached Sage Medhas.

“Revered sage,” Suratha asked, “why does the mind cling to things even when we know they have hurt us?”

Medhas smiled gently.

“There is a power within this world that can cloud even the understanding of wise people,” he explained. “The sages call her **Mahamaya**—the great divine power. She can veil understanding, and she can also grant knowledge.”

“Who is this great power?” asked Samadhi.

“She is the Divine Mother,” said Medhas. “Let me tell you what happened when even the gods needed her help.”

And the sage began his story.

*Illustration moment: The king and merchant seated under a banyan tree, listening while the forest slowly gives way to the memory of a divine kingdom.*

## Chapter 2 · Two Kings Take Over the Heavens

The gods had once fought a terrible enemy called **Mahishasura**, a powerful Asura who could change his form. The Divine Mother had defeated him. After the victory, she promised the gods that when danger returned and they remembered her, she would come to their aid.

That promise soon mattered again.

Two brothers, **Shumbha** and **Nishumbha**, rose to great power. They were **Asura kings**—rulers of mighty clans that often fought the gods for authority over the worlds.

They were proud of their strength. Their armies were enormous. One by one, they took control of the positions held by Indra and other gods. They claimed treasures, royal elephants, horses, and the portions of sacred offerings that had belonged to the gods.

Indra and the others were driven from their places of power.

“Are we to stand by while they take everything?” one of the gods asked.

“We have fought,” another answered. “They have defeated our armies.”

Then someone remembered the promise of the Divine Mother.

“She told us to call upon her when we were in danger.”

The gods journeyed to the Himalayas. There they prayed to the Mother, remembering her as the power behind knowledge, courage, life, and every living being.

**Why didn't Shiva or Vishnu simply end the war?** In this particular sacred story, the gods have been displaced and turn to the Mother because of her earlier promise. The tale is told to reveal her supreme power; it does not say that Shiva or Vishnu had lost all divine strength because of a specific boon granted to these brothers.

*Illustration moment: A proud Asura court laden with treasures, contrasted with dispossessed gods praying among the mountain peaks.*

## Chapter 3 · The Goddess on the Mountain

While the gods were praying, **Parvati**, the mountain-born Goddess, came to the river to bathe.

“Whom are you praising?” she asked.

A shining divine form emerged from her. It was the Mother answering the gods' prayer.

This Goddess is called by several names in the story. **Ambika** means the Mother. **Kaushiki** is the name she receives because she emerged from Parvati. **Chandika** describes her fierce power when she confronts evil. These are not three unrelated heroines in this episode: they refer to the central Goddess facing the Asura kings.

For the rest of our story, we will usually call her **the Mother**, and use **Chandika** when she is leading the battle.

Far away, two Asura commanders named **Chanda** and **Munda** saw her radiant form upon the mountain. They served King Shumbha, and their task was to observe and report what they found.

They hurried back to the palace.

“Great king,” Chanda said, “we have seen someone unlike anyone we have ever seen before.”

“All the finest treasures of the worlds are already in your court,” Munda added. “Yet the Goddess on the mountain shines brighter than them all.”

Shumbha had grown accustomed to treating everything he desired as something that should belong to him.

He sent an envoy named Sugriva to the mountain with a proposal: the Goddess should marry Shumbha, or his brother Nishumbha, and share their wealth.

The Mother listened calmly.

“Tell your king,” she answered in this retelling of her vow, “that I have made a promise. Only someone who defeats me in battle and matches my strength may become my husband.”

Sugriva was offended. He warned her that the Asura kings would force her to obey.

The Mother did not withdraw her words.

**That was how a proposal turned into the beginning of a war.** The Asura kings wanted to possess and command her; she would not be taken by force.

*Illustration moment: The Goddess serene on a Himalayan peak, an envoy standing before her, then a sharp cut to the brothers receiving her answer.*

## Chapter 4 · The Two Commanders and the Birth of Kali

Shumbha was furious when he heard the envoy's report.

He first sent a great commander named **Dhumralochana** with a large force. Dhumralochana threatened to drag the Mother away. When he charged, the Goddess's powerful cry reduced him to ashes, and her lion scattered his army.

Shumbha sent Chanda and Munda next.

They were not minor strangers: they were the same commanders who had discovered the Goddess on the mountain. Now they led soldiers to capture her. Their danger lay in their royal authority, their armies, and their weapons—not in a special magical boon described in this chapter.

The soldiers climbed the mountain with bows drawn and swords raised.

The Mother sat upon her lion. She saw the attackers approaching.

Then her expression changed.

From her frowning forehead appeared **Kali**, a fearsome divine form. She carried powerful weapons, and her roar filled the sky. She rushed into the invading forces.

Chanda and Munda tried to overcome her. They attacked with weapons and fierce determination, but Kali defeated them both.

She brought news of her victory to the Mother.

“Because you have defeated Chanda and Munda,” the Mother told her, “you shall be known as **Chamunda**.”

And so **Kali and Chamunda are names of the same fierce form in this part of the story**. Kali was not another Goddess who had been waiting offstage without introduction.

Back in the Asura capital, the news spread.

Chanda and Munda had fallen. Their soldiers had been defeated. But Shumbha and Nishumbha still ruled powerful armies.

“We will send more,” Shumbha ordered.

*Illustration moment: Kali emerging from Ambika's forehead; a brief action sequence; Shumbha hearing the news in his court.*

## Chapter 5 · The Mothers Gather

Shumbha called the great Asura clans and their armies together. Some were called Daityas, some Kambus, and others had different clan names. For our story, it is enough to know that **many powerful Asura forces were now marching as one army**.

The mountains trembled beneath the sound of their approach.

Chandika stood on her lion, ready to meet them. Kali was with her.

Now something extraordinary happened.

From the divine power of **Brahma, Shiva, Vishnu, Skanda and Indra**, radiant female forms appeared. They came with the weapons and vehicles associated with those deities. The sacred text calls them the **Mothers**—powerful forms of divine Shakti.

Why had they come? **The Asura armies were gathering against the Mother, and these divine powers appeared to join the battle against the enemies of the gods.** Later in the story, the Mother explains that these many powers are her own manifestations.

First came **Brahmani**, associated with Brahma. She rode in a vehicle drawn by swans and carried prayer beads and a water-pot.

Then came **Maheshwari**, associated with Shiva, riding a bull and carrying a trident.

**Kaumari**, associated with Skanda, came on a peacock, ready with her spear.

**Vaishnavi**, associated with Vishnu, arrived on Garuda, bearing a conch, discus and other weapons.

**Varahi** appeared in the powerful boar-like form linked with Vishnu's Varaha manifestation.

**Narasimhi**, the fierce form connected with Narasimha, came with lion-like features and a tremendous roar. The Sanskrit describes the movement of her **mane** as scattering the stars themselves—an extraordinary sacred image that does not need to match ordinary zoology.

Last came **Aindri**, associated with Indra, riding an elephant and holding his mighty thunderbolt.

Seven divine powers had gathered. They were joined in battle by Kali and the Mother herself.

Suratha interrupted the sage.

“Are these seven new goddesses separate from the Mother?”

“They appear as distinct powers in this battle,” Medhas answered in our adapted conversation. “Keep listening. Near the end of this great war, the Mother herself will explain their unity.”

The king leaned forward. He wanted to know what would happen next.

*Illustration moment: A series of spacious paired portraits, giving each Mother an unmistakable silhouette, then a full spread as they join the battlefield.*

## Chapter 6 · A Messenger Named Shivaduti

As the seven Mothers assembled, another fierce power appeared **from Chandika herself**.

Her cries sounded like a hundred jackals. The Goddess sent **Shiva** to deliver a final warning to Shumbha and Nishumbha.

“Return the worlds to Indra and the other gods,” the warning said. “Go to the lower regions if you wish to live. But if you insist on war, be ready for battle.”

Because this fierce Goddess made **Shiva her messenger**, she became known as **Shivaduti**, meaning **she whose messenger is Shiva**. She is a distinct manifestation in this scene, not simply a new name dropped into the story without an introduction.

The warning was rejected.

The Asura armies attacked.

Brahmani cast her water upon the enemy. Those it touched lost strength and courage. Maheshwari's trident struck the advancing ranks. Kaumari moved with her spear; Vaishnavi answered with her discus.

Varahi charged through the enemy with tremendous boar-like force. Narasimhi caught enemies with her claws and fought amid the battle cries, her wild lion-like mane shaking as she moved. Aindri hurled her thunderbolt. Kali fought fiercely, and Shivaduti's terrible laughter unsettled the invaders.

The Asuras began to retreat.

The Mothers were winning.

And then a new warrior stepped forward.

*Illustration moment: Shiva carrying the warning; Shivaduti's emergence; the Mothers' distinct abilities in battle, including Narasimhi; a shadowed figure about to enter.*

## Chapter 7 · The Warrior Called Raktabija

The warrior was named **Raktabija**.

He belonged to the Asura side fighting for Shumbha and Nishumbha. When he saw their soldiers retreating before the Mothers, he advanced to face the divine forces himself.

In this chapter of the *Devi Mahatmya*, **we are not told who his parents were, how he grew up, or which deity—if any—gave him a boon**. So we will not invent a secret origin for him.

What the story does tell us is remarkable.

Raktabija carried a heavy club and possessed an extraordinary power: **whenever a drop of his blood fell on the ground, a new Asura of his size and strength sprang up from that drop**.

The Mothers did not yet know how dangerous that power would be.

Aindri faced him first.

She raised her thunderbolt and struck him.

Blood fell to the ground.

A new warrior rose from the earth.

Then another.

Then many more.

Suratha stared at the sage.

“Wait,” he said. “Did Aindri's weapon fail?”

“No,” answered Medhas. “It wounded him. But that was exactly the problem.”

Every drop created another enemy.

*Illustration moment: Aindri's first strike, close-up of one falling drop, then a three-panel reveal as one new figure becomes many.*

## Chapter 8 · When Fighting Makes the Enemy Stronger

The Mothers continued their attack.

Vaishnavi's discus struck Raktabija. More blood fell. More warriors appeared.

Kaumari attacked with her spear. Maheshwari struck with her trident. Varahi fought with her own weapons. Each wound created new Asuras wherever the blood reached the earth.

The new fighters did not merely stand there. They joined the battle immediately, armed and strong.

Soon the space between the chariots was crowded. Every gap in the enemy's formation filled with new warriors. What had looked like a victory began to turn into a disaster.

Above the battlefield, the gods grew frightened.

At the hermitage, Samadhi could not keep quiet.

“They are brave and strong,” he said. “But the harder they fight, the greater his army becomes!”

“Yes,” Medhas replied. “And they cannot simply stop defending themselves.”

“What can they do?” Suratha asked.

The sage paused.

“The Mother saw what everyone else was missing.”

It was not enough to strike Raktabija.

**They had to prevent his blood from reaching the ground.**

*Illustration moment: A battlefield increasingly crowded by identical warriors, the gods looking on in alarm, then a close-up of Chandika watching a drop of blood.*

## Chapter 9 · The Mother's New Plan

Chandika turned to Kali—the fierce Goddess who had defeated Chanda and Munda and earned the name Chamunda.

She gave her a clear instruction.

“Catch his blood before it reaches the earth,” she told her in this adapted dialogue. “And stop the warriors who rise from it. I will keep fighting him.”

Kali moved swiftly across the battlefield.

Chandika struck Raktabija. As his blood flowed, Kali caught it before it could reach the ground. Any new warriors that arose were swallowed before they could spread across the field.

Now something had changed.

The Mothers' weapons were still powerful, but the results were different.

No new army was rising with every wound.

Raktabija kept fighting. He swung his heavy club at Chandika, but the blow did not hurt her. The Goddess answered with more weapons while Kali continued her work.

The great Asura weakened. His blood no longer fed an endless army. At last, Raktabija fell.

The gods rejoiced. The Mothers celebrated their victory.

The battle had been won not by ignoring the enemy's strange ability, but by understanding exactly how it worked.

*Illustration moment: Chandika and Kali coordinating across the battlefield, falling droplets intercepted in mid-air, Raktabija's power finally broken. Keep the action dramatic but suitable for family reading.*

## Epilogue · What Happened Next?

The forest became quiet again.

Suratha watched the evening light fading through the trees. Samadhi sat beside him, thinking about the story.

“The Mothers were not weak,” said the merchant. “But the way they first fought made the danger worse.”

Medhas nodded.

“The Mother understood what was causing the enemy's strength to multiply.”

Suratha looked up.

“Raktabija has fallen,” he said. **“What did Shumbha and Nishumbha do when they heard of this defeat?”**

Medhas began to answer.

For the two Asura kings, the war was not over.

And our next book begins with their reply.

## Why this story is told during Navaratri

The *Devi Mahatmya*, also called the *Durga Saptashati* or *Chandi Path*, is a central text in many Navaratri and Durga Puja observances. The text itself speaks about listening to the Goddess's story during the great annual worship in autumn (chapter 12). This Raktabija episode is **one part of the larger story** of the Goddess overcoming powerful Asuras. It is **not** a claim that Raktabija was defeated on one particular day of Navaratri or on Vijayadashami.

Different regions and temple traditions honour the Goddess through different daily forms, recitations and rituals. Our illustrated book is a companion to that wider celebration.

## A Quick Guide to the People in This Story

- **The Divine Mother / Ambika / Kaushiki / Chandika:** The central Goddess who answers the gods' prayers; these names identify her in different parts of this episode.
- **Kali / Chamunda:** The fierce form who emerges from Ambika's forehead and receives the name Chamunda after defeating Chanda and Munda.
- **Shumbha and Nishumbha:** Powerful Asura brothers who take over the gods' positions and demand that the Goddess submit to them.
- **Chanda and Munda:** Commanders serving the brothers. They first report seeing the Goddess and are later sent to capture her.
- **Raktabija:** A great warrior on the Asura side whose blood produces new fighters when it touches the earth.
- **The Seven Mothers:** Brahmani, Maheshwari, Kaumari, Vaishnavi, Varahi, Narasimhi and Aindri—divine powers who appear to join Chandika in battle.
- **Shivaduti:** A fierce power emerging from Chandika, named because she sends Shiva as her messenger.
- **Suratha:** A king who has lost his kingdom and listens to Sage Medhas.
- **Samadhi:** A merchant driven away by his family who listens beside Suratha.
- **Medhas:** The sage who tells them the stories of the Divine Mother.

### What do “Asura,” “Daitya” and “Rakshasa” mean?

**Asura** is a broad term for a class of powerful beings frequently opposed to the gods in these stories. **Daitya** often means an Asura descended from Diti. **Kambus** here is the name of a particular clan or group mentioned among Shumbha's forces. **Rakshasa** is another category in Indian narrative traditions; it is not simply a synonym for every Asura. For younger readers, we use **Asura warriors** unless a particular lineage matters to the plot.

### Why does Narasimhi have a mane?

The sacred text portrays Narasimhi as resembling Narasimha, the man-lion divine form, and describes her tossing a mane so powerfully that stars are scattered. This is poetic sacred iconography, not a zoological claim about ordinary lionesses. Our art can show feminine features together with a stylized mane and make the textual reason clear.

### What this volume does not invent

This episode does not explain Shumbha and Nishumbha's power through a specific origin boon, or give Chanda, Munda or Raktabija an invented supernatural backstory. Other texts and traditions may give additional accounts, but they must be attributed separately and not silently inserted into this version.
